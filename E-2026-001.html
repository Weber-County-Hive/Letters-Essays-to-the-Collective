Power and restraint

Where Were You — And What Were We Actually Founded On

A memory of September 11, 2001, and what the founders were actually fighting: a trade and tax system the people paying into it had no say in.

By Shauna Miller · 6 min read
First published on The Weber County Hive Substack, Sep 11, 2026 · Posted here Sep 25, 2026 · Last updated Sep 25, 2026 · Page look updated Nov. 2, 2026

IN PLAIN WORDSThis is a personal essay. The views are the writer’s own and are kept here, apart from the Hive’s records-only reporting. Its factual statements were checked the same way reader pieces are: 13 checked, 3 corrected, 1 removed. Every check and change is listed at the end.

Where I was

I was in my living room, eating Frosted Mini Wheats, watching the Today Show, when the first plane hit. I watched the second one live.

I called my boyfriend — now my husband — right away. He hadn’t heard yet. He was driving a semi-truck, and I was in tears because he’d already signed up for boot camp, set to leave on October 15th. Without a second of hesitation, he told me not to worry — that the military had been a choice, and now it wasn’t one he could turn away from.

I went to work at the Ogden Credit Bureau. When they announced the Pentagon had been hit, I wanted to climb under my desk. Our boss came into a room full of women with kids in daycare and school that day and told us America was going to be okay. Within the hour, a helicopter landed next to our building, and men in black hurried someone into a waiting car. We told each other: if they brought someone important here, Utah must be safe — they wouldn’t have landed here otherwise.

That day I will never forget.

For a few weeks in the fall of 2001, this country did something it almost never does: it stopped being divided against itself. Congress sang on the Capitol steps. Flags went up on houses that had never flown one. It didn’t last — it never does — but it happened, and enough of us remember it happening that it’s worth asking what it actually took to get there, and why it takes something like that instead of just... deciding to.

What we were actually founded on

Before this country was one people reacting to an attack from outside, it was thirteen colonies full of people who had stopped believing their own government answered to them.

George Washington experienced that loss of control through his own plantation business. Virginia tobacco was not being sold in a genuinely open international market. British trade laws restricted where colonial tobacco could go and kept the crop moving through the British imperial system. Washington sent his tobacco to Robert Cary & Company, a London merchant house that sold the crop, purchased the British goods he ordered, deducted shipping and merchant charges, and maintained the account.

That meant Washington did not control both sides of his own transaction.

His tobacco went out through a market Britain restricted. The merchant reported what the crop had brought. British goods came back through the same account, often purchased on credit. If the tobacco proceeds did not cover the merchandise, freight, merchant charges, and existing balance, the difference did not disappear. It was carried forward as debt, with interest accumulating.

The math looked something like this:

Tobacco sale proceeds
minus freight and merchant charges
minus imported goods purchased on credit
equals a balance carried forward as debt

Washington complained in 1765 that his tobacco sales were “pitifully low” next to what neighbors’ crops brought, while for many of the goods bought for him he paid “exceeding heavily.” Poor harvests and Washington’s own expensive purchases contributed to the problem too, but the restricted market left him with little bargaining power. According to Colonial Williamsburg, he eventually owed Cary & Company approximately £800, with interest accumulating at 5 percent.

The Crown did not necessarily dictate the price written beside every individual barrel of tobacco. It did something more structural: it restricted the market in which the sale occurred. The merchant controlled the sale price and kept the account, while the planter bore the risk when the numbers did not work out.

Washington eventually moved away from tobacco and toward wheat—something he could sell closer to home without depending as heavily on a British merchant. That decision was economic, but it was also about independence.

Layered on top of this restricted trade system were Parliament’s direct taxes and duties—the Stamp Act, the Townshend duties, and the tax on tea. Colonists were not simply objecting to the amount they paid. They were objecting to a system that controlled their trade, taxed their transactions, and gave them no representation in the Parliament making those rules.

Economic control was not the only cause of the American Revolution. But it turned the founders’ arguments about liberty into something people experienced in their crops, purchases, accounts, and debts. The fight was not only over what government collected. It was over whether the people living under the system had any meaningful power over how that system worked.

That’s what actually started this. Not an abstract idea about liberty. A trade and tax system designed so the people paying into it had no real say in how it worked.

What the people who built the next system warned us about

The men who won that fight spent the rest of their lives worried the country would build a version of the same trap for itself.

Washington, in his 1796 Farewell Address, warned about political factions that “distract the public councils and enfeeble the public administration,” about piling up debt and “throwing upon posterity the burden which we ourselves ought to bear,” and — in the line most often quoted — about permanent foreign alliances.

Jefferson, in 1816, wrote to a friend that he believed “banking establishments are more dangerous than standing armies,” and, in a separate letter that year, hoped the country would “crush in it’s birth the aristocracy of our monied corporations which dare already to challenge our government to a trial of strength” (spelling as in the original) — not corporations as a legal form, but the concentration of power he saw forming around them.

Lincoln, at 28, warned that the real danger to the country “must spring up amongst us” rather than arrive from abroad. At 52, as president, he wrote that “Labor is prior to and independent of capital. … Labor is the superior of capital.”

None of them were talking about a single villain. They were describing a pattern: a government and an economic system that could, left unwatched, stop answering to the people paying into it — the same complaint that started the Revolution in the first place.

Where that leaves us now

State and local governments spent an estimated $45 billion on business tax incentives in 2015, about $12 billion of it in property-tax abatements alone, according to the Lincoln Institute of Land Policy — some broadly available, some individually negotiated. Until a 2015 accounting rule, GASB Statement 77, governments weren’t required to report what their tax-abatement agreements cost them. Whether any specific deal is a legitimate tool for growth or a modern version of the arrangement Jefferson worried about isn’t something a number by itself can answer.

It’s worth being honest about what’s different, too. Colonists had no vote, no representation in the Parliament that taxed them, and no court that would hear their cases. Americans today have all three, imperfect as they are — which is either the reason this comparison doesn’t really hold, or the reason it’s more alarming when the same complaints show up anyway. That’s a real disagreement, and it’s not this piece’s job to settle it for you.

What isn’t in dispute is the thing 2001 proved: this country knows how to stop being divided against itself when it decides the moment calls for it. The founders’ whole argument was that it shouldn’t take a moment like that. The Revolution wasn’t started by people who’d run out of patience with each other. It was started by people who’d run out of patience with a system that no longer answered to them.

Twenty-five years after one Tuesday reminded us what we look like unified, it’s worth asking what it would take to look like that on a Tuesday when nothing has happened at all.
