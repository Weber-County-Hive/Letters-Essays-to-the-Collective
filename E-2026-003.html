Choosing sides

Both Sides Are Playing Us

Each party’s stated first principle gets suspended exactly where holding to it would cost them power. The same scrutiny, applied to both.

By Shauna Miller · 14 min read
First published on The Weber County Hive Substack, Aug 15, 2026 · Posted here Sep 25, 2026 · Last updated Sep 25, 2026

IN PLAIN WORDSThis is a personal essay. The views are the writer’s own and are kept here, apart from the Hive’s records-only reporting. Its factual statements were checked the same way reader pieces are: 18 checked, 6 corrected, 0 removed. Every check and change is listed at the end.

My issue isn’t with individual Republicans. It’s with what the Republican Party has become — and, in many ways, what it has been for decades. That said, this isn’t a one-sided brief: the same scrutiny gets applied to Democrats before this is over, because the pattern I’m describing isn’t a party flaw, it’s a power flaw.

We’re constantly told the GOP is the party of fiscal responsibility. Yet, since Reagan, Republican administrations have repeatedly overseen ballooning deficits and rising debt, and several ended with severe economic crises that required major federal intervention. “Fiscal responsibility” loses its meaning when the books never seem to balance.

We’re also told they’re the party of limited government. Limited enough to stay out of the boardroom, perhaps — but not out of your doctor’s office. It’s an odd definition of liberty when government is invited into some of the most personal decisions a family can make. It’s “limited” to a degree, with the caveat of sacrificing individual liberties while espousing the exact opposite.

When many finally read Project 2025, they reacted as if it were some shocking new blueprint for extremism. Many of its major policy goals build on decades of conservative movement priorities, while some proposals go substantially further than prior Republican administrations attempted. Donald Trump didn’t invent most of those ideas — he simply proved willing to push them further and faster than many of his predecessors. One explanation for why so many Republicans who dislike Trump personally continue to support him politically is that he advances policy goals they’ve long supported.

Here’s the question we should all be asking: Is Trump/MAGA truly the problem, or is he simply the first Republican willing to say the quiet part out loud and pursue the agenda the party has been building toward for years?

But I don’t want to let one side off the hook by only aiming this at the other. If the goal is honest reckoning rather than tribal scorekeeping, both parties deserve the same scrutiny, applied with the same rigor. My argument is this: each party’s stated first principle gets suspended exactly where holding to it would cost them power. That’s not “both sides are equally bad” as a lazy shrug — it’s a specific, testable claim about hypocrisy as a bipartisan structural feature, not a personality flaw of one side.

Each section below also breaks out MAGA as its own category, separate from “Republican” generally. That’s not a stylistic choice — traditional GOP conservatism operated inside institutional norms and free-market rhetoric, even when it fell short of them. MAGA functions differently: it’s a populist movement organized around personal loyalty and institutional distrust rather than party platform, and it has broken with older GOP orthodoxy on real substance (trade, foreign intervention, industrial policy), not just tone. Treating the two as identical would erase that difference; treating MAGA as the only version of the GOP would erase the decades of pattern that came before it.

Why I Left, and Why I Haven’t Landed Anywhere Else

I didn’t leave the Republican Party because I stopped believing in the things it claimed to stand for. I left because the actions stopped matching the words. You can only hear “fiscal responsibility” and “limited government” so many times while watching neither actually happen before the words lose all meaning. At some point it wasn’t a party anymore — it was a script, and I stopped being willing to perform my part in it.

But I haven’t become a Democrat, and this piece is exactly why. Swapping one script for another isn’t a solution — it’s just changing which hypocrisy you’re willing to tolerate. Both parties talk about representing voters while running on money that comes from somewhere else entirely: corporate donors, PACs, industries with a direct stake in the legislation those same officials will vote on. That’s not a Republican problem or a Democratic problem. It’s the operating system underneath both of them. And both parties have gotten very good at using division itself as a tool — keeping people angry at each other is easier, and more profitable, than being accountable to either side.

So this isn’t a piece written from the other team’s bench. It’s written from outside both dugouts, watching the same game get played under two different jerseys.

The Money Underneath Both Jerseys

If there’s one thread that ties every section below together more than any single policy area, it’s this one — and it’s the reason I’d argue the deficits, healthcare, and veterans failures aren’t really separate stories. They’re symptoms of the same funding structure.

Citizens United (2010), followed two months later by the D.C. Circuit’s SpeechNow.org v. FEC, transformed campaign finance together — the first allowed unlimited independent spending by corporations and unions, the second struck down contribution limits to groups that only make independent expenditures, and together they created the modern Super PAC. Both parties have built their fundraising machinery around that framework since — not just Republicans, despite Citizens United’s reputation as a conservative-aligned decision. Corporate PACs, industry associations, and outside super PACs now fund incumbents and challengers in both parties, often the same donors giving to members on both sides of the aisle on issues where their industry has a stake.

The revolving door between government and industry runs through both parties — former members of Congress and agency staff moving into lobbying roles for the industries they used to regulate, and lobbyists moving into government positions overseeing those same industries. It’s a structural feature of Washington, not a partisan one.

Defense contractors fund campaigns and maintain lobbying operations that shape procurement and war-funding decisions regardless of which party controls Congress — a bipartisan pattern that helps explain why defense budgets rarely shrink no matter which party promises fiscal discipline.

Pharmaceutical lobbying has slowed drug-pricing reform under both parties — Democrats who campaign on lowering drug costs and Republicans who campaign on free-market healthcare have both, at different points, watered down or delayed legislation that industry lobbying opposed.

The 2008 Wall Street bailouts, and the industry-friendly regulatory rollbacks that followed, are maybe the clearest example of the pattern: Republican and Democratic administrations both supported and implemented rescue measures for the financial industry — TARP was signed under Bush and largely administered under Obama — in a way neither party has managed for veterans’ care, mental health infrastructure, or child welfare funding.

None of this is conspiracy. It’s incentive. Large donors are substantially more likely to receive access to policymakers than ordinary voters are, and that access shapes whose priorities get attention. That’s not a Republican failure or a Democratic one — it’s the actual operating system both parties run on, and it’s why “vote the other party in” has never been the fix people hoped it would be.

Deficits and the Size of Government

Republicans campaign as the party of fiscal discipline, yet Bush 43’s 2001 tax cut and Trump’s 2017 tax cut were both projected by Congress’s nonpartisan scorekeepers (the Congressional Budget Office and the Joint Committee on Taxation) to reduce revenue and increase deficits, and Reagan’s 1981 tax cut was followed by large deficits. The Congressional Budget Office projected in 2018 that the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act alone would add about $1.9 trillion to deficits over 2018–2028, including added interest costs. On “limited government,” the same party that objects to regulation in the boardroom has supported reaching into some of the most personal decisions a family can make: reproductive care, gender-affirming care for minors, bathroom access. Government shrinks or grows depending on whose freedom is at stake.

Democrats aren’t exempt from the fiscal version of this. COVID relief, infrastructure spending, and student loan forgiveness attempts have also added significantly to deficits, often while campaigning on fiscal responsibility relative to the GOP — the target of the spending is different, but the gap between rhetoric and budget reality shows up on both sides. The nonpartisan Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget estimated Trump’s full term approved about $8.4 trillion in new ten-year borrowing versus roughly $4.7 trillion approved over Biden’s full term — both presidents added trillions, just at different scale and through different mechanisms (tax cuts and COVID relief for one, COVID relief and spending bills for the other). And the party that brands itself as the defender of civil liberties had its own COVID-era tension: extended school closures beyond what evidence supported in some states, particularly after vaccines became widely available, and mandates enforced through employment or access restrictions, drew genuine civil-liberties criticism from within the Democratic coalition itself.

MAGA specifically has been more willing than traditional GOP orthodoxy to expand deficits deliberately — the 2017 tax cuts, 2020 stimulus, tariff policy that functions as a hidden consumer tax — while still deploying “fiscal responsibility” rhetoric against opponents. It has also been more willing to use federal power aggressively where it serves the movement — the alternate-elector slates organized across multiple states after the 2020 election, which the federal indictment calls “fraudulent slates of electors,” the pressure campaign on Georgia election officials to “find” additional votes, the pressure on Vice President Pence to reject certified electors, and the events of January 6, 2021 are the clearest examples — alongside proposed federal reach into local curriculum and library content, and expanded executive authority claims. That’s a more assertive version of “limited except where it matters to us” than earlier GOP framing.

Healthcare

Republicans oppose single-payer as government overreach while having spent decades defending the employer-based system — itself a product of WWII-era wage controls, not a free-market outcome, that happens to benefit insurers and employers. The GOP ran on repealing the ACA for a decade, took full control of government in 2017, and couldn’t pass a replacement — suggesting the “free market alternative” was more rhetorical than real. Several GOP-led states also rejected ACA Medicaid expansion on ideological grounds. The federal government covers 90% of expansion costs, and KFF’s review of the research found that Medicaid expansion reduced hospitals’ uncompensated care costs compared with states that did not expand — the ideological stance came with a real, documented fiscal tradeoff.

Democrats ran on Medicare for All or a public option in multiple cycles, but delivered market-based reform (the ACA) that preserved private insurers when given full control in 2009 and 2021 — often citing “political reality” after campaigning on the larger promise. Drug pricing reform took decades to pass and remains narrow relative to the rhetoric around it.

MAGA has not articulated a comprehensive healthcare policy comparable to earlier Republican reform proposals — Trump’s first term didn’t produce a replacement for the ACA despite years of promising one. Messaging has shifted from “here’s our plan” toward general institutional distrust: skepticism of public health agencies and vaccines. That’s a distinct posture from the traditional GOP’s market-based healthcare argument.

Veterans

Republicans pair strong pro-military, pro-veteran rhetoric with budget proposals that have, at times, proposed slowing VA funding growth or shifting care toward privatization under an “efficiency” banner — even as outcomes data on privatized care has been mixed, and even though other GOP-led budgets have expanded VA spending. The party most likely to support intervention abroad has also been slower to fund the long-tail costs of war: the PACT Act, addressing burn-pit exposure, took nearly two decades of advocacy and drew significant GOP opposition in 2022 before finally passing — opposition that senators leading it framed as objecting to the bill’s budget mechanism (reclassifying $400 billion from discretionary to mandatory spending) rather than the underlying veteran care expansion, though veterans’ groups argued the distinction delayed care regardless of the stated reason.

Democrats champion universal social programs, but veteran-specific care has still seen chronic backlogs under Democratic administrations too — the 2014 VA wait-time scandal happened under Obama, showing the gap between “we believe in strong public services” and actual agency performance when veterans aren’t the electoral centerpiece.

MAGA’s version of this is arguably the most performative — strong symbolic identification through rally staging and imagery — set against reported comments disparaging wounded or fallen service members, originally reported by Jeffrey Goldberg in The Atlantic in September 2020 and later confirmed on the record by former White House Chief of Staff Gen. John Kelly in an October 2023 statement to CNN. Trump has denied the account. Whatever the final read on that dispute, it reflects a personalized tension between symbolism and substance that goes beyond a generic funding critique.

Mental Health

Republicans shift the conversation from firearms policy to mental health after mass shootings, but that rhetorical pivot hasn’t been matched with proportional funding or access — it functions as deflection more than commitment, and often ties mental illness to violence in ways most research doesn’t support — a 2014 study published by the American Psychological Association found that only 7.5% of crimes committed by people with serious mental illness were directly related to their symptoms. Enforcement of mental health parity laws has stayed weak, and Medicaid expansion refusal in many GOP-led states has cut off treatment access in exactly the rural areas hit hardest by opioid and suicide crises.

Democrats passed parity legislation but enforcement has remained weak even under full Democratic control — a legislative win substituting for the funding and follow-through needed to make it real. Some criminal justice reforms reduced incarceration for low-level, mental-illness-linked offenses without building out equivalent community treatment infrastructure first, leaving people cycling between the street, the ER, and jail.

MAGA has leaned harder into the “mental health, not gun laws” deflection than the GOP baseline, often combining it with stigmatizing, violence-coded language rather than treatment-forward framing — worsening stigma while offering even less policy substance.

Child Predators

Republicans invoke child safety heavily in cultural debates — drag shows, school curricula, bathroom policy — while some GOP-aligned religious institutions and figures have had documented failures of their own. The Southern Baptist Convention’s own 2022 independent investigation by Guidepost Solutions found the denomination mishandled abuse allegations for years. Josh Duggar — son of Jim Bob Duggar, a former Arkansas state representative, and a former executive director of the Family Research Council’s political arm — was convicted in December 2021 of receiving child sexual abuse material (a possession count was vacated before sentencing) and sentenced in May 2022 to 151 months in federal prison; in 2015, after a police report surfaced, his parents said he had inappropriately touched several of his sisters as a teenager and he apologized, saying he had “acted inexcusably,” and some prominent Republican figures, including Mike Huckabee, publicly defended the family after that history became known. Funding for child protective services and foster care remains chronically thin nationally, including in states that campaign heavily on child-safety messaging.

Democrats have had their own institutional reckonings — within institutions culturally aligned with the Democratic coalition, including parts of the entertainment industry, certain elite schools, and some public districts — where allegations were known internally before public accountability followed, echoing the pattern criticized on the religious-institution side. Some criminal justice reform efforts around sentencing discretion have created friction with victim-advocacy groups on child exploitation cases specifically. And prominent Democratic politicians have generally maintained closer financial and political relationships with major tech platforms — Meta and pre-Musk Twitter among them — which sits uneasily next to those platforms’ documented, sometimes slow-walked responses to child exploitation material.

MAGA is the most complicated case here, because it’s also the movement that turned child-trafficking rhetoric into a significant grassroots organizing tool — real energy, even where fringe-associated. At the same time, Trump had a documented Palm Beach social association with Epstein in the 1990s and into the early 2000s, on the record in a 2002 New York Magazine profile in which Trump called him a “terrific guy” — years before Epstein was indicted in 2006 and pleaded guilty in 2008 to state charges including procurement of minors to engage in prostitution. Trump has said publicly, including in a July 2019 Oval Office statement after Epstein’s federal arrest, that the two had a falling out and hadn’t spoken in roughly 15 years. MAGA-aligned officials at state and local levels have also faced their own abuse allegations, same as any political movement’s officials do. The gap here isn’t underfunded rhetoric — it’s the loudest rhetorical energy on the issue coexisting with the same institutional-accountability failures as everyone else.

Where Did the Party I Knew Go?

It’s tempting to answer that question by pointing to the growing pool of independents as proof that people are fleeing both parties in search of something better. That’s a satisfying story, but it isn’t quite what the data shows — and it’s worth getting right, because the real number tells a sharper story than the myth does.

Independent identification isn’t stalled. It’s at a record high — 45% of U.S. adults called themselves political independents in 2025, according to Gallup, up from 43% in 2014, 2023, and 2024, and the highest in Gallup’s polling since 1988. Republicans and Democrats are tied at 27% each. Younger Americans are driving much of the shift: a majority of Gen Z and Millennials now decline to claim either party label.

So the number is growing. What isn’t growing is a functioning alternative to go with it. Most people who call themselves independent still vote consistently for one party once you account for which way they “lean” — political scientists sometimes call these hidden partisans. Third-party and independent candidates haven’t gained real electoral traction to match the identity shift. The exit door is wide open; there’s just nowhere real to walk through it.

That’s the more honest version of “where did the party I knew go.” People aren’t wrong to feel it disappearing — the data backs that instinct up. But the response isn’t a growing center that both parties will eventually have to answer to. It’s a growing number of people quietly opting out of a label while the two-party structure — primaries, ballot access laws, campaign finance, media coverage built around a binary — keeps that exit mostly symbolic. People are leaving the parties faster than the system is giving them anywhere else to go.

Maybe that’s the real story worth telling: not that one party is worse than the other, but that both have drifted from whatever they once claimed to stand for, and the growing refusal to pick a side is itself the most honest political statement being made in this country right now.

None of this lets voters entirely off the hook, either. The system only works because we participate in it. Politicians respond to incentives, and those incentives come from us as much as they come from donors. As long as we reward outrage more than honesty, slogans more than consistency, and tribal loyalty more than accountability, neither party has much reason to change.
