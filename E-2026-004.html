Choosing sides

Red Rover

Two lines of kids, arms locked so tight your fingers went white. What the COVID years cost, and why neither team deserves our locked arms.

By Shauna Miller · 10 min read
First published on The Weber County Hive Substack, Oct 2, 2026 · Posted here Oct 2, 2026 · Last updated Oct 2, 2026

IN PLAIN WORDSThis is a personal essay. The views are the writer’s own and are kept here, apart from the Hive’s records-only reporting. Its factual statements were checked the same way reader pieces are: 8 checked, 4 corrected, 0 removed. Every check and change is listed at the end.

Red Rover, Red Rover, send someone right over.

You remember how the game actually worked. Two lines of kids, arms locked so tight your fingers went white, daring the other side to send their fastest kid running at you. If you broke through, you got to take someone back to your line. If you didn't, you joined theirs. Either way, somebody hit the ground.

I think that's the game we're all playing now. Except nobody's laughing on the way down, and nobody's picking teams for fun anymore.

Where I was standing

I studied healthcare administration. We talked about pandemic scenarios in that program — not as science fiction, as planning. My husband is a veteran. He deployed to Iraq once, and I carried a specific, quiet fear the whole time that most military spouses don't have to name out loud: germ warfare. Over the years his service also took him through Saipan, Guam, South Korea, Japan — postings that came with their own long list of required vaccinations, mosquito-borne illness among them. By the time COVID showed up, getting another shot wasn't a new idea to either of us. So when COVID hit and people started saying maybe this came out of a lab in China, maybe it got out on purpose, I didn't hear a conspiracy theory. I heard something I'd already been trained to consider possible. I was vocal about it early, and I still believe it.

I'm not writing this to relitigate where the virus came from. I'm writing this because of what happened to all of us while we argued about it.

What it actually cost

My father died in an industrial accident at 49. Sudden. No warning, no chance to say the things you save for later. So when COVID started taking people while their families waited outside a window, unable to hold a hand or say I love you one more time while a heart was still beating — that wasn't a policy debate to me. That was my worst fear, playing out for strangers, over and over, on the news.

My kids paid for it too, in the particular ways kids pay for things adults are arguing about. My daughter missed huge stretches of high school. My son lost a friend — a kid he'd gamed with online, twelve years old, gone. My youngest had just come back from years of homeschooling and all she wanted in the world was to be in a classroom with her friends, and instead she got more walls.

My husband worked for the railroad through all of it. If he got sick, he had to prove he caught it on the job to get paid time off — while the company was housing him in crew hotels where hygiene was, generously, not a priority. Given everything his service had already required of his body, COVID was one more shot on a long list, and we made the call to do what we could to protect him. My doctor told me I should get one too. At McKay-Dee, because of my MS, they didn't even put me in the same line as my husband — different shot, different protocol, worked out between us and a nurse holding a clipboard.

I have a close family member who ran a healthcare facility in Texas, one of the first in the state to record a COVID case in a long-term care setting. The scrutiny he came under — from the governor's office, on down — was awful, and underneath it was a much harder reality nobody was reporting: staff who were terrified to even walk into the COVID-positive zone, no one lining up to take their place, and the families of the people inside still needing someone to answer for all of it. He was the one who had to hold that. Nobody made room for what that actually required of one person.

The freedom that wasn't quite real

We moved to Florida in the middle of all this, and the story everywhere was that Florida meant freedom — the governor said you get to choose, and people heard that as no rules here. That wasn't actually true, and living it taught me something the headlines didn't.

Our HOA pool was closed at first. People were furious about it. Then the other side was mad it was open. Choice: you don't go.

Our daughter's middle school had stricter protocols than anything we'd seen in Utah — full plexiglass partitions for kids under twelve. Costco here checked IDs and enforced the rules more strictly than the Costco we'd left in Utah. My neighborhood went quiet in a way that unsettled me — nobody walking, nobody outside, a kind of stillness I hadn't expected from the state that was supposed to be the one saying come as you are.

Here's what the governor's "you get to choose" line actually meant: he left the real decisions to school districts and to companies. Some of those companies required the vaccine — not because the state made them, but because their employees had already agreed to vaccination as a term of employment when they took the job, sometimes years before COVID existed. So people who'd said yes to a job requirement in 2018 found themselves boxed in by that same yes in 2021, in a state that was telling the country it didn't do that here.

He got to look like a hero to the people who hated every mandate — while healthcare workers in this state never actually got the freedom he was selling. His administration sued the federal government over the CMS vaccine mandate covering hospitals, nursing homes, and hospices, and lost. The courts let the federal rule stand, so healthcare employers were stuck between two sets of rules: state law required them to offer broad exemptions, the federal rule required staff vaccination with narrower ones, and their Medicare and Medicaid funding was on the line either way. That's a neat trick if you can pull it off: take the credit for freedom in your press releases while the people actually working in that system stayed exactly as boxed in as before.

And to be honest about who's who in that fight: that mandate was Biden's, start to finish. It was issued under his administration in November 2021 and wasn't lifted until June 2023, also under Biden. Trump wasn't in office for any of it and had no hand in stopping it or extending it. I'm not interested in writing something that blurs who actually did what just because it's tidier to blame one villain — that's exactly the kind of team-picking this whole essay is against.

But Trump has his own version of the same move, just on different subjects. In 2020, after briefly claiming "total authority" to reopen the country, he backed off and let reopening be a state-by-state decision. On vaccines, he did the opposite — Operation Warp Speed was entirely federal, over $12 billion in direct federal investment, the Defense Department helping run distribution, Trump's own administration picking which vaccine candidates got funded. No deferring to states there at all. And now, in his second term, immigration enforcement gets the same full-federal treatment: executive orders overriding local sanctuary policies, moves to cut off federal funding to cities and states that limit cooperation with ICE, and 27 federal lawsuits against more than 20 states, counties and cities by July 2026. States decide when it's a decision he doesn't want to own. Washington decides when it's one he does.

"Freedom state" didn't mean simple, either. Enrolling my own kids in school meant Florida had to verify our Utah shot records were real — over six weeks before my kids could even set foot in a classroom. I was never once allowed inside the building myself, not for enrollment, not for anything. I did all of it from a chair at the district office.

That's where I met a boy who'd just moved from New Jersey. Both of his parents were dead, and he was starting over with a relative he'd go live with now. I sat next to him in that hallway with my own six-week paperwork problem and I cried — not for me, for him. Whatever chaos I was navigating, his was a different order of magnitude — and he was doing it alone, waiting like everyone else.

That's the same gun-that-wasn't-a-gun problem in different clothes. Utah had its version. Florida had its version. Neither one was the free choice either side's messaging made it sound like.

We didn't just take my word for any of this. We took the kids to thirteen states during COVID to see for ourselves what each one was actually like, versus what the news said. New Orleans matched its coverage the closest. We hit national and state parks along the way, too — that part wasn't research, that was just us trying to have a good year in a bad one.

The gun that wasn't in anyone's hand

I chose to get vaccinated. Nobody held a gun to my head.

Feed your family or lose your job.

Nobody pointed a gun at most Americans. But millions felt like the world had one pointed at them anyway.

That's not nothing. That's real pressure, and I'm not going to pretend it wasn't. But it's also not the same as force. You did have a choice. It was just a choice with a cost attached, and some people paid that cost and some people didn't, and I don't think either group deserves the contempt they got from the other side.

I also watched, in real time, how little room this country makes for something that's cheap and actually works. Trump got mocked hardest for hydroxychloroquine and for musing about disinfectant and light inside the body. The ridicule arrived before the evidence did. Later studies largely did not support hydroxychloroquine as a COVID treatment. To me, both facts matter. Public debate shouldn't get ahead of the evidence, and evidence shouldn't be ignored once it arrives. Look at marijuana for the cleaner version of the same rot: something almost anyone could grow in a backyard, decades spent making sure it stayed illegal or nearly impossible to access, especially somewhere like Utah. That's not a coincidence. That's what happens whenever the answer doesn't require a prescription, a patent, or a purchase. That has always made me wonder whether our incentives are aligned with our values.

I'm not saying every cheap idea works. I'm saying they deserve to be examined on the evidence, not dismissed because they don't fit neatly into the systems we've already built.

The lines that never got answered

Dr. Fauci sat in front of the nation and pled the Fifth. It's his right, same as anyone's. Trump did the identical thing in 2022, more than 400 times in a single deposition in the New York attorney general’s civil investigation. Watch what happens next: half the room says this proves I was right about him and goes quiet about their own guy doing the exact same thing three feet away. The other half runs the same play in reverse. Nobody stops to ask the real question — why does the person we're supposed to be able to trust with the truth keep landing on the one answer that isn't the truth?

Look at the Epstein files. No justice there either — not close. Look at the world. China is not going to be held accountable for what it did, or didn't do, in Wuhan. Nobody with real power, on either team, has actually answered for any of it. Meanwhile we've got people who lost parents to a virus, parents who refused the vaccine and called it "their time" — and I don't know if it was. None of us do. We don't get to know what would have happened in a world without this virus in it. That not-knowing is exactly why the certainty on both sides makes me so tired.

Quit locking your arms

Our politics has become remarkably good at playing us against each other.

So here's where I land, standing in the yard looking at both lines.

Both sides play us against each other. Both sides have a Fifth Amendment moment they'd rather you forget about. Both sides have a "your body, your choice" argument they only apply to the topics that are convenient for them. Both sides have decided that admitting fault is the same as losing, so nobody admits anything, and the rest of us get run over in the crossfire.

I chose the vaccine. I'd make that choice again. I still think this virus had an origin story nobody in power wants fully investigated. Both of those things are true at once, and neither one makes me a member of a team.

I'm done holding my arms so tight that anyone who tries to cross gets knocked flat. I'd rather be the kid who lets someone through. Not because I don't believe in anything — I believe in plenty. But because somewhere in all this, we forgot that the point of the game was never to keep everyone out. It was to see who was brave enough to run.

Where are the people who can still do that?
