Choosing sides

Does Your Tribe or Integrity Come First?

In a world of Gadiantons, doing the right thing means dropping the tribe.

By Shauna Miller · 2 min read
First published on The Weber County Hive Substack, Aug 29, 2026 · Posted here Sep 25, 2026 · Last updated Sep 25, 2026

IN PLAIN WORDSThis is a personal essay. The views are the writer’s own and are kept here, apart from the Hive’s records-only reporting. Its factual statements were checked the same way reader pieces are: 3 checked, 1 corrected, 0 removed. Every check and change is listed at the end.

I grew up on the Tennis Shoes Among the Nephites series. Like thousands of kids raised in Utah, I devoured Chris Heimerdinger’s time-travel adventures, tracking Jim, Jenny, and Garth as they ducked spears in ancient jungles and faced off against corrupt cabals.

The books were fun, but the lesson underneath was heavy: Gadianton robbers weren’t just guys hiding in caves with secret handshakes.

They were a systemic disease. They were leaders who used righteous-sounding language, leveraged backroom influence, and relied on a docile, trusting public to look the other way while they consolidated power and wealth. We were taught to recognize that pattern, fear it, and resist it at all costs.

Eventually, life took me down a path where I stepped away from the LDS faith. But stepping outside the culture did something unexpected: it gave me a vantage point to see the very mechanics I’d been warned about as a child playing out in broad daylight across Utah’s political landscape.

The deepest irony of living in a culture saturated with Book of Mormon warnings about societal decay is watching the exact same decay tolerated—and often defended—by the people holding the text.

In modern state and local politics, we see the classic blueprint:

  • Closed-door deals and cozy insider networks rationalized under the banner of “protecting our values.”
  • Politicians using pious rhetoric as a bulletproof vest against basic accountability.
  • Citizens giving bad actors a permanent free pass simply because the politician shares their party label, their neighborhood, or their faith.

The Book of Mormon itself explicitly warns that the downfall of a community rarely comes from external enemies—it comes from the quiet, internal decay of ethics when good people decide that loyalty to the team matters more than loyalty to the truth.

The book’s own narrators — Mormon, who compiled it, and his son Moroni, who finished it — practically break the fourth wall to warn future readers that they would become blind to the very corruption growing in their own backyards.

When you call out this kind of tribalism, the knee-jerk reaction from defenders is predictable. They want to attack the messenger: “They’re bitter,” or “They left, so they just don’t get it.”

But you don’t have to believe in the theology to recognize structural hypocrisy. In fact, if leaving taught me anything, it’s that integrity doesn’t belong to an institution or a party. Real integrity is the willingness to look at “your side”—the candidate you voted for, the developer in your circle, or the official you know—and say: This is wrong.

If we only resist corruption when it wears the other side’s jersey, we aren’t standing for principle. We’re just playing for a team.

It’s easy to read stories about heroically standing against ancient cabals. It’s a lot harder to stand up in a county commission meeting, call out a bad land deal, or refuse to vote for a corrupt local politician who quotes your favorite scriptures on Sunday and sells out your community on Tuesday.

In a world full of Gadiantons—and a culture full of people blind to them—doing the right thing means having the courage to see clearly, speak plainly, and drop the tribe.
